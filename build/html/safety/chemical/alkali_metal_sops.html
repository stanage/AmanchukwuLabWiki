Alkali Metals

Alkali metals (lithium, sodium, and potassium) are vital components of our laboratory’s battery and catalysis research pipelines. Due to their extreme reactivity with air, moisture, and acids, these elements present significant fire, explosion, and toxicity hazards if mishandled. This page serves as a centralized protocol for their safe manipulation within the glovebox, the strict environmental controls required for catalysis testing, safe quenching practices, and compliant waste disposal for both bulk metal scraps and assembled coin cells.

General Alkali Metals Usage Guidelines

1. Battery Group Procedures

  • Separation: Maintain strict separation between alkali metal types; never cross-contaminate Li, Na, and K tools or disposal jars.

  • Storage Limit: Do not leave extra alkali metal loose in the glovebox; keep excess stored in your designated bin.

  • Contamination Prevention: Always use a paper towel or aluminum foil as a mat when working to prevent glovebox floor contamination. Throw away the mat after use, or quench it if it shows signs of contamination.

  • Handling Na and K: Peel off the polymer coating, wrap the metal in a Kimwipe, and quench it in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) before disposing of it in solid waste.

  • Handling Li: Cut and polish pieces on a cutting board kept inside the glovebox, and throw extra pieces directly into the Li waste jar.

  • Decrimping: Place contaminated Na in the Na waste jar and K in the K waste jar.

  • Cell Component Sorting: Keep contaminated coin cell parts in the glovebox disposal jar, but remove uncontaminated parts to quench in IPA.

  • Tool Maintenance: Bring any contaminated tools out of the glovebox for immediate quenching.

2. Catalysis Group Procedures

  • Storage Location: Potassium (K) is stored in GB2.

  • Preparation: Before a reaction, remove a K piece, strip the polymer coating, and set the coating aside for quenching.

  • Cell Configuration: Submerge the K chunk in the electrolyte within a small cell to minimize headspace. Use a PTFE toothpick or inert material to keep the K securely at the bottom of the cell.

  • Transfer: Seal the vial and wrap it in parafilm before removing it from the glovebox.

  • Buddy System: Never perform this reaction alone; at least one other person must be present in the lab.

  • Emergency Preparedness: Keep a sand bucket directly next to the fume hood during setup, reaction, and breakdown.

  • Cell Set Up in the Fume Hood:

    • Conduct electrocatalysis reactions in the back fume hood by the balcony (the same space used to quench alkali metals).

    • Ensure no hydrogen tanks or solvent bottles are in the fume hood during the reaction.

    • Connect the inlet valve to a carbon monoxide (CO) tank equipped with a flame arrestor.

    • Ensure the CO inlet needle goes directly into the electrolyte, controlled by an attached flowmeter.

    • Place the outlet valve needle in the gas headspace, connected to a mineral oil bubbler (or hexane if lower viscosity is needed for pressure relief).

  • During the Reaction:

    • Never leave the running reaction unattended.

    • Use clear signage indicating the presence of K metal and CO bubbling.

    • If stepping away briefly, fully close the sash and post a sign explicitly forbidding others from touching the fume hood.

  • End of Reaction:

    • Lower the CO flow to 1 sccm and remove the inlet needle with the flow still on to prevent air infiltration.

    • Remove the outlet needle, reseal the vial heavily with parafilm, and transfer it to the glovebox for analysis.

    • Dispose of excess K in the glass K-waste container in the glovebox and retain the solid from the K surface for analysis.

Quenching Protocol (All Lab Members)

Quenching is a highly hazardous procedure that must be performed carefully in the open-space fume hood by the balcony.

Warning

Solvent Selection Warning: Always use Isopropyl Alcohol (IPA) for Na and K, as it is a weaker proton donor than ethanol or water. This results in a slower reaction, producing less heat and less hydrogen gas, significantly lowering the risk of fire.

  • Hood Preparation: Clear the fume hood of all fire hazards, including Kimwipes, solvent bottles, and samples, before transferring items from the glovebox. Wipe the fume hood floor to remove any residual solvents.

  • Bath Setup: Prepare a beaker bath with sufficient solvent to fully submerge the items: use IPA for Na/K, and DI water strictly for Li only.

  • Safety Positioning: Position the sand bucket immediately adjacent to the quenching fume hood.

  • Tool Quenching: Submerge tools until bubbling ceases entirely, then remove and store them properly.

  • Coin Cell Quenching: Submerge parts in the appropriate solvent, remain present until bubbling stops, cover the beaker with aluminum foil, label it clearly, and leave it overnight. Dispose of parts in solid waste the next day.

Danger

Emergency Fire Protocol: If a fire starts, immediately shovel sand onto the beaker to stifle the flame, or use the yellow Class D fire extinguisher located by the office. If the fire grows out of control, evacuate the lab immediately and call campus security at 773-702-8181.

Alkali Metal Waste Disposal SOP

  • Containment: Collect all alkali metal waste in air-tight jars inside the glovebox, strictly separating Li, Na, and K into their own designated jars.

  • Segregation: Do not place general coin cell parts into these jars; only extract and add the alkali metals themselves.

  • Exception: Stainless steel spacers with irretrievably stuck metal may be placed in the jar with mineral oil, as quenching them poses a high fire risk.

  • Scrap Volume: Once a jar accumulates 1 lb of metal (approximately ¾ full), completely cover the metal scraps with mineral oil. Ensure Li scraps are fully coated before sealing, as Lithium floats in mineral oil.

  • Sealing: Seal the jar tightly with parafilm to prevent air infiltration.

Warning

Potassium Warning: Keep potassium waste in the glovebox until EHSA physically arrives for pickup, as K soaked in mineral oil can still form an impact-sensitive explosive superoxide layer if exposed to trace oxygen.

  • Transport Prep: Upon removal from the glovebox, place the sealed jar in a properly labeled secondary container indicating that the contents spark in air/water (and are impact-sensitive for K). Securely pack the glass jars inside the secondary container using chemical delivery packaging to prevent shifting or breaking during transport.

  • Post-handling Clean: Quench any tools that touched the metals: use DI water for small Li quantities, IPA for large Li quantities and all Na/K, followed by ethanol for Na/K, and a final DI water rinse.

Coin Cell Disposal Protocol

  • Insulation: Wrap tested, properly crimped coin cells completely in tape to eliminate electrical connection risks in the waste bin.

  • Cell Inspection: Only tape and dispose of cells that are perfectly sealed with no volume expansion or bulging.

  • Compromised Cells: If a cell exhibits bulging or poor crimping, decrimp it, separate the components into the solid chemical waste, and extract the alkali metal into the glovebox waste jar.

  • Sorting Stream: Sort waste into three separate bins: Li metal/Stainless Steel cells, Na metal cells, and K metal cells. Place the tape-wrapped coin cells into their respective disposal bins and send them with EHSA for disposal.

  • Electrolyte Waste Profile: The internal electrolyte category contains a wide array of solvents and salts, including EC, DEC, DMC, FEC, DOL, DME, FDMB, \(LiFSI\), \(LiTFSI\), \(CsFSI\), \(KFSI\), PEO, perfluoropolyether, \(Li_3PS_4\), and LLZO.

Note

Reference Material: Additional information on safe alkali metal usage can be found via the Stanford EHS Alkali Metals Reference Guide.
